Punch Has No Character, And Meddeb Just Made a Strategic Mistake

Slim El Fatmi · May 16, 2026

There's a rule in branding that most companies learn the hard way: you don't abandon equity, you build on it.

Meddeb broke that rule. Punch has no character, no origin signal, no cultural tension. It looks like someone studied every soda bottle on the Tunisian market and designed the average. The bottle is generic. The name is generic. And in a category where distinctiveness is the only defensible position, that's a serious problem.

The Presta Bottles Are Mythic. And That's Not a Metaphor.

The little glass Presta Meddeb bottles are the kind of product that earns cultural status through decades of consistent context, not through marketing campaigns.

They are linked, in the Tunisian collective memory, to saunas and refreshment. To the smell of old grocery stores. To the visual language of classic Tunisian fast foods, the kind of places that still have hand-painted signs and don't take cards. This is not nostalgia. This is brand equity at its most durable: sensory, situational, and deeply authentic.

Cidre Meddeb isn't just a beverage. In brand equity terms, it functions as national heritage. That's a category of asset most brands spend decades trying to construct and never reach.

The move to plastic was not inherently wrong. Plastic has distribution reach that glass can't match, supermarkets, vending machines, kiosks, petrol stations. The logic is sound.

The name change and packaging erasure? That's where it goes wrong.

Marketers Hate Tension. Great Ideas Feed On It.

Most marketers, when faced with a brand paradox, try to resolve it by eliminating one of the poles. The creative move, the strategic move, is to hold both poles and design through the tension.

Here, the tension is precise: How do you move to plastic without destroying authenticity?

This is not a communications problem. It's a design problem. And it has been solved before.

The curvy silhouette of the Presta bottle is a distinctive brand asset. It carries the tactile memory of the glass format. It triggers recognition before the consumer reads a single word. Erasing it in the transition to plastic doesn't simplify the brand, it amputates it.

The correct brief was never "design a new plastic bottle for a new brand."

It was: "How do we preserve the perceptual signature of Presta Meddeb in a plastic format?"

The Real Value Was Never in the Taste

This is the insight that makes everything else make sense.

Cidre Meddeb is not taste-differentiated. It's experience-differentiated.

The perceived value it delivers is not a flavor note, it's an emergent phenomenon built from multiple converging signals:

The curvy silhouette of the bottle, which reads as crafted, not manufactured. The small opening of the glass bottle, which controls the flow, shapes the drinking ritual. The old-school graphic design of the posters and labels, which signals decades of continuity. The distribution context, old groceries, Tunisian fast foods, which frames the entire experience as authentic before the first sip.

Remove any of these, and the emergent perception collapses. The taste alone cannot carry the weight. No taste can.

What Meddeb was selling, whether they knew it or not, was a perception of craftsmanship and authenticity, delivered through glass, through old graphics, through the specific places where the product appeared.

The Graphic Design Insight: Make It More Old School, Not More Modern

Here's the move that nobody seems to be considering.

The problem with plastic isn't the material, it's the perception the material creates. Plastic reads as industrial. Mass-produced. Interchangeable.

But graphic design can work as a parasite on that perception.

If the label, the typography, the color palette, the illustration style are sufficiently old-school, sufficiently craft-coded, they can mute the plastic signal entirely. The eye reads the whole object. If the graphic language screams "1960s Tunisian craftsmanship," the plastic body becomes secondary information.

This isn't speculation. This is exactly what Sprite did.

They called it Contour PET Design, an explicit attempt to transfer the sensory memory of the iconic contoured glass Coca-Cola bottle into the plastic format. Not by changing the material. By making the plastic bottle feel like the glass bottle through shape, curve, and tactile design.

For Meddeb, the equivalent move would be: Preserve or translate the curvy silhouette into the plastic mold, even a subtle nod to the original shape preserves recognition. Design the label to maximum old-school intensity, vintage typography, archival color palettes, illustration styles that signal pre-industrial production. Keep the Meddeb name and Presta heritage visible, these are the assets that carry the equity, not the word "Punch."

The Strategic Bottom Line

Punch is what happens when you have a brand problem and solve it with a product launch instead of a brand decision.

Meddeb had one of the most genuinely differentiated assets in Tunisian food culture, not because of a superior formula, but because of decades of accumulated context, sensory association, and cultural embedding.

The transition to plastic was an opportunity to extend that asset into new distribution channels while keeping the equity intact.

Instead, the new name erases the origin. The new packaging erases the shape. The new visual identity erases the graphic language that carried the authenticity signal.

What's left? A generic soda with a generic name and a generic bottle, competing in a category where being generic is a death sentence.

The irony is that solving the real tension, how to preserve Presta Meddeb's soul in plastic, would have been a genuinely interesting brand story. One worth telling. One the Tunisian market would have recognized and responded to.

That story is still available. But every month that passes under the Punch identity makes it harder to go back.
