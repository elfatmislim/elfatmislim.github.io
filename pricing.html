When Pricing Goes Wrong: The Bottle That Lied to Your Brain

Slim El Fatmi · May 25, 2026

There's a product sitting in Tunisian supermarkets right now that commits one of the most fascinating pricing errors in recent local marketing memory. You probably remember it. The campaign was loud, well-produced, and genuinely exciting. Chef Wafik lent it his credibility in February 2022. The message was bold: "inzel al jben." Expectations were high.

Then came the price tag: 7,100 millimes.

Total dissonance.

The Campaign Did Everything Right, Then the Bottle Undid It

Let me be clear: the February 2022 launch campaign was not a failure of creativity. Partnering with Chef Wafik was a smart credibility move. The "inzel al jben" positioning was genuinely differentiated — it wasn't selling cheese spread, it was selling real cheese in a convenient format. The aspiration was premium. The promise was premium.

But the moment you see the bottle, something happens in your brain that no amount of copywriting can override.

The Inference the Brand Never Accounted For

The bottle is a squeeze bottle. Soft, flexible, designed for dispensing.

Your brain doesn't read bottles. It pattern-matches them. And the pattern your brain retrieves, automatically, involuntarily, before you've read a single word on the label is: sauce.

Ketchup. Harissa. Mustard. Condiment.

This is not a conscious judgment. It's a heuristic. A mental shortcut shaped by decades of product encounters. The bottle triggers the category, and the category triggers a value anchor. Sauce is a condiment. Condiments are support players. They are, by definition, inferior to the main ingredient.

And what is the main ingredient here? Cheese.

So the brain has now constructed a hierarchy: cheese is the essence, the bottle is sauce-like, sauce is subordinate to cheese, therefore, this product is worth less than cheese.

You can print "100% fromage" on the label in the largest font your designer will allow. It doesn't matter. Reading is a deliberate act. System 2. Slow, effortful, conscious. The bottle inference is automatic. System 1. Fast, associative, unconscious. By the time you're reading the label, the brain has already filed its verdict.

A 7,100 millime price point cannot survive that verdict.

This Is the Error

The product asked the consumer to pay a premium price for something their brain had already categorized as a condiment derivative. The cognitive dissonance isn't a perception problem, it's a structural one. The container contradicts the positioning.

It's the equivalent of serving a gastronomic dish in a takeaway box and wondering why customers resist paying restaurant prices. The vessel communicates value before the content gets a chance to.

The Fix Isn't Marketing. It's Industrial Design.

You cannot solve a System 1 problem with a System 2 message. More advertising, better copy, a stronger endorsement — none of it will rewire the sauce inference as long as the squeeze bottle remains.

The fix is the bottle.

Retire the squeeze format. Move to a contoured HDPE dairy bottle, the kind with ergonomic shaping, a solid structure, a form language that says dairy product rather than condiment. Yes, you sacrifice the squeeze functionality. That's the trade-off, and it's the right one to make. Functionality that undermines perceived value is not a feature, it's a liability.

A structured dairy bottle kills the sauce inference at the source. It repositions the product visually, before any claim is made, before any chef endorses it, before any consumer reads a single word. The brain sees dairy. The brain prices dairy. The 7,100 makes sense.

The Broader Lesson

Pricing is not just a number. It's a claim about value. And that claim will always be tested against everything else the product communicates — including its container, its shape, its material, and the automatic associations those physical properties trigger in the human brain.

When your packaging speaks a different language than your price, the packaging wins. Every time.

The brain is not a rational calculator. It is a pattern-recognition machine running on heuristics built from a lifetime of sensory experience. Market to the machine, or the machine will market against you.
